<Paragraph>I've spent quite some time working with datasets, cleaning data, writing SQL queries, and building visualizations. But the more I worked with data, the more curious I became about the part that happens before the analysis:</Paragraph>

<Highlight>How do we collect data from different sources, prepare it, and make it available for analysis?</Highlight>

<H1>First, What Exactly Is ETL?</H1>

<Paragraph>Think of a restaurant: ingredients arrive each morning from the farm, butcher, and market, but they can’t be served as-is. You gather them, wash, cut, and cook them, then plate and serve.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>That is exactly what ETL does with data. ETL stands for:</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "**Extract**: Collect the raw data from where it lives",
    "**Transform**: Clean, organize, and prepare the extracted data.",
    "**Load**: Store it in a final place where people can use it"
]}/>

<Figure src={EtlPipeline} alt="Kitchen analogy for ETL: raw ingredients arrive from different places, get washed and cooked in a kitchen, and leave as a finished meal."/>

<Paragraph>In the image, ingredients arrive from different places, get washed and cooked in the kitchen, and leave as a finished meal. Swap ingredients for data, the kitchen for the pipeline, and the meal for your database — that is ETL.</Paragraph>

<H1>First, a few words you will keep hearing</H1>

<Paragraph>Before we go deeper, let's decode the common terms.</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "**Data** – Any piece of information: a customer's name, an order amount, a click on a website",
    "**Database** – A database is an organized digital collection of information stored on a computer so it can be quickly found, changed, and updated",
    "**Data warehouse** – A data warehouse is a centralized system designed specifically to store large amounts of data from different sources for reporting, business intelligence, and analysis.",
    "**Source** – Wherever the data comes from: a database, a spreadsheet, a website, an app.",
    "**Destination** (target) – Wherever the data ends up, usually a data warehouse.",
    "**Pipeline** – A series of steps that data travels through automatically, like water through connected pipes. An ETL pipeline is simply the E, T and L steps linked together."
]}/>

<H1>Why do we even need ETL?</H1>

<Paragraph>In most companies, data is scattered everywhere. Sales sit in one system, customer details in another, website visits in a third. Each one uses different formats, different names for the same thing, and sometimes has mistakes.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>If you try to analyse this mess directly, you get wrong answers. ETL solves this by:</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "Bringing data from many places into one place",
    "Cleaning mistakes and removing duplicates",
    "Making everything follow one consistent format",
    "Saving time by running automatically, instead of someone copying data by hand"
]}/>

<Paragraph>Now, let's understand what actually happens at each stage.</Paragraph>

<H2>Stage 1: Extract - Collecting the Data</H2>

<Paragraph>Extraction is the first step of an ETL process.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>The purpose is simple: collect data from its original source and bring it into the pipeline.</Paragraph>

<Highlight>Where does this data come from:</Highlight>

<BulletList items={[

    "APIs (a way for one app to politely ask another app for its data)",
    "Websites and apps"
]}/>

<H2>Ways to Extract Data</H2>

<Paragraph>There are 2 different ways to extract data.</Paragraph>

<NumberList items={[
    "Full Extraction",
    "Incremental Extraction"
]}/>

<H3>Full Extraction</H3>

<Paragraph>As the name suggests, full extraction retrieves the entire dataset from the source.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example, if a table contains 10,000 orders, a full extraction retrieves all 10,000 records.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>This approach is simple and can be useful for smaller datasets</Paragraph>

<H3>Incremental Extraction</H3>

<Paragraph>Instead of retrieving everything, incremental extraction retrieves only new or modified records since the previous extraction.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example, if we already extracted 10,000 orders and another 200 orders were added, we only need to retrieve those 200 new records.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>This can save time and resources when dealing with large and frequently updated datasets.</Paragraph>

<H2>Stage 2: Transform - Making the Data Useful</H2>

<Paragraph>Now that we've collected the data, is it ready for analysis?</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>Not always.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>Data coming from different systems may have different formats, missing values, duplicate records, inconsistent naming conventions, or incorrect data types.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example, imagine an e-commerce company receives the following customer records:</Paragraph>

<H2>Stage 3: Load - Storing the Prepared Data</H2>

<Paragraph>We've collected the data and prepared it. What's next?</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>Now we need somewhere to store it.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>This is the responsibility of the loading stage.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>Loading takes the transformed data and transfers it into a destination, such as:</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "A relational database (MySQL, PostgreSQL)",
    "A data warehouse (Snowflake, BigQuery, Amazon Redshift)",
    "A cloud storage destination (Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Amazon Web Services (AWS))"
]}
/>

<Paragraph>For example, our e-commerce company might load its prepared customer and order data into a MySQL database so that analysts can query it using SQL.</Paragraph>

<H2>Different Loading Approaches</H2>

<Paragraph>Just like extraction, loading can also happen in 2 different ways.</Paragraph>

<NumberList items={[
    "Full Load",
    "Incremental Load"
]}
/>

<H3>Full Load</H3>

<Paragraph>In a full load, the entire dataset is loaded into the destination.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example, every time the pipeline runs, the existing destination data might be replaced with the latest complete dataset.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>This can work well for smaller datasets or initial loads.</Paragraph>

<H3>Incremental Load</H3>

<Paragraph>In incremental loading, only new or modified records are inserted or updated in the destination.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example:</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "10,000 orders are already stored",
    "200 new orders arrive",
    "15 existing orders have been modified"
]}
/>

<Paragraph>Instead of reloading everything, the pipeline processes the 200 new and 15 modified records.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>This approach is generally more efficient for large datasets.</Paragraph>

<H1>What Happens After Loading?</H1>

<Paragraph>Once the data reaches its destination, it becomes available for downstream processes.</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>For example:</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "Data Analysts can query it using SQL",
    "BI tools can connect to it to build dashboards",
    "Data Scientists can use it for further analysis and modelling"
]}
/>

<Paragraph>The loading stage essentially makes the prepared data accessible to the people and systems that need it.</Paragraph>

<H1>ETL vs. ELT: What's the Difference?</H1>

<Paragraph>While learning about ETL, you'll also come across another term: ELT (Extract, Load, Transform).</Paragraph>

<Paragraph>Both approaches involve collecting data, preparing it, and making it available for analysis. The main difference is when and where the transformation happens.</Paragraph>

<BulletList items={[
    "In ETL, data is transformed before it reaches the destination",
    "In ELT, data is first extracted and loaded into the destination. Transformation happens afterward, usually within the destination system."
]}
/>

<Paragraph>For example, a company might load raw customer and order data directly into a cloud data warehouse and then use SQL to clean, combine, and prepare the data there.</Paragraph>
